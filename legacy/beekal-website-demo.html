One place to answer “where is my order?”

Garment exporter · Dhaka · 180 staff · Beekal Build

The problem
Buyers asked for order status daily. The answer was rebuilt by hand each time — spreadsheets, email, a walk to the floor.
Before
Four Excel files. One WhatsApp group. Two merchandisers lost most mornings chasing updates — and once quoted two buyers different ship dates for the same order.
Diagnosis
Not a reporting problem — a record problem. No single order record existed anywhere; every department kept its own copy.
What Beekal changed
One order record, sample to shipment, owned by merchandising. Milestones are entered once on the floor; buyers see that same record, read-only.
How it was built
A web app with role-based access, a tablet entry screen for line supervisors, and a nightly export into their existing accounting package. Three milestones, fourteen weeks.

Result

~2 hoursof each merchandiser's morning returned to actual merchandising
One ship dateper order, visible to the buyer and the factory at the same time
No spreadsheetsorder status is no longer maintained in Excel by anyone

LessonThe tablet had to survive gloves and weak wifi. Next time we prototype on the factory floor in week one, not week five.

A 2011 desktop system, opened up instead of replaced.

Pharmaceutical distributor · 12 depots · Beekal Modernize

The problem
The distribution system still did its job — but nothing could connect to it, and month-end close ate three days of manual work.
Before
A .NET desktop app on one server. Depot stock arrived as emailed spreadsheets. Any new report meant a change request to a developer who’d already left.
Diagnosis
The logic was sound, tested by a decade of use. The problem was the closed architecture: no API, reports hard-coded into the client, direct database access the only way in.
What Beekal changed
The logic stayed. We wrapped it in a documented API, moved reporting onto a read replica, and swapped the emailed spreadsheet for a browser form.
How it was built
An API layer over the existing database, a read replica for reporting, depot web forms, then a staged cutover — one depot at a time, across a quarter. No big-bang migration.

Result

3 days → same daymonth-end close, because reporting no longer waits for the desktop client
12 depotsreporting stock through one form instead of twelve spreadsheets
Zero rewritesof the business logic that already worked

LessonDepot managers trusted their spreadsheet. Adoption only moved once we let them keep it running alongside the form for the first month.

The same shipment, entered once instead of three times.

Freight forwarder · 40 staff · Beekal Automate

The problem
Every shipment was typed into the carrier portal, the operations tracker and accounting. The three never quite agreed, and invoices went out from whichever was open.
Before
~60 shipments a week, re-keyed across three systems. Corrections often surfaced at invoicing — sometimes found by the customer first.
Diagnosis
A data-ownership problem, not a discipline problem. No system was authoritative, so careful staff kept a private fourth copy to stay safe.
What Beekal changed
The portal became the single source for shipment data. Operations and accounting now receive it automatically; anything that doesn’t match gets flagged for a person, not retyped by one.
How it was built
An integration service between the portal, the tracker’s API and accounting, plus a daily reconciliation report listing only mismatches. Two weeks to build, two running in parallel before switch-off.

Result

~6 hoursof re-keying removed from each operations person's week
One sourceof shipment truth, with accounting fed from it automatically
Exceptions onlystaff review a short mismatch list instead of every record

LessonThe reconciliation report mattered more than the integration. Seeing what didn’t match is what made people stop keeping their private copy.

Ten years of tender documents, finally answerable.

Engineering services firm · 90 staff · Beekal AI

The problem
Engineers burned hours hunting through old proposals, specs and drawings for answers that already existed — somewhere on the file server.
Before
40,000+ documents spread across a file server, email and two SharePoint sites. New engineers asked senior ones, who answered from memory.
Diagnosis
Not only a search problem. The documents had no consistent structure, and the answers people needed sat inside PDFs and scanned drawings that search couldn’t read.
What Beekal changed
An internal assistant that answers from the firm’s own documents and shows the source page for every answer — so an engineer can check it before it goes in a tender.
How it was built
A document pipeline (OCR, chunking, indexing), a retrieval assistant that cites its sources, access rules mirroring existing folder permissions, and a review loop where engineers flag weak answers.

Result

Every answer citedno answer is shown without the source document behind it
Permission-awarestaff see only the documents they could already open
One pilot teamproved the value before anything was rolled out firm-wide

LessonWe shipped “show me the source” before answer quality was even good. That’s what earned trust — without it, the pilot gets switched off.

After launch, the system kept getting better.

B2B software platform · Beekal Care

The problem
The platform launched well, then drifted. Nobody owned performance, security updates, or the small improvements users kept asking for.
Before
Faults were reported by customers, not detected. Security updates waited for a quiet week that never came. The roadmap was whatever was loudest that month.
Diagnosis
Not a skill gap. There was no standing time, and no owner, for the work that’s never urgent — right up until it is.
What Beekal changed
A monthly cycle: monitor, patch, ship one improvement from the request list, and review the roadmap together with the client.
How it was built
Uptime and error monitoring with alerts, a patch schedule, a fixed monthly improvement budget, and a one-page review each month: what changed, what’s next.

Result

Detected firstfaults are found by monitoring, not reported by customers
One improvementshipped every month from the user request list
Monthly reviewa written record of what changed and what is next

LessonThe one-page monthly review did more for the relationship than the engineering did. Clients stop worrying once they can see what happened.

Questions people ask before they book

What does it cost?

A fixed price, agreed in writing before we start. It depends on the size of your business and how many systems are involved — no hourly billing, no surprises on the invoice.

What if it doesn’t turn up anything useful?

The first thing we do is map exactly where your time and money are leaking, so that’s unlikely. But if we don’t think an assessment is the right next step for your business, we’ll say so on the first call — before any money changes hands.

How long does it take?

Two to three weeks, kickoff to roadmap review — scheduled around your operations, not the other way round.

How much of our time do you need?

About six hours total: interviews with four to six people who do the work, a systems walkthrough, and one final review. We do the rest without you in the room.

Do we have to build with you afterwards?

No. The roadmap names systems and priorities, not vendors — another team can act on it. We’d rather be chosen for the build than assumed into it.

Is what we share kept confidential?

Yes. We sign a mutual NDA before the first interview. Nothing you share ever appears in a case study without your written approval.

We already know what we want built. Do we still need this?

Not always. If your requirements are written down and everyone agrees on them, we can go straight to a proposal. The assessment is for when priority isn’t settled — or when people would describe the problem differently.

How we work

    Understand
